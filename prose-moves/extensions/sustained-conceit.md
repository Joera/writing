# Sustained conceit

## Use when
- A whole comparison (one system against another) maps closely onto an everyday one, part for part.
- The text is an essay or explainer, and figuration allows a central extended analogy.

## Don't use when
- `figuration: literal`. This rules it out for the Polyphony white paper.
- The analogy breaks at a point the argument depends on.

## Conflicts with
- `figuration: literal`; [everyday-analogy](everyday-analogy.md) and [working-metaphor](working-metaphor.md) in the same section (one image system per section).

## Tags
`figuration: sustained conceit` (a value added with Stephenson; see [properties.md](../properties.md)).

## Safe or adds
**Adds.**

## The move
Pick one everyday comparison and extend it across several paragraphs, mapping each part of the real system onto a part of the analogy. Push it until it becomes slightly absurd, then say plainly where it breaks. The absurdity is where the reader remembers it.

## Before
> Polyphony is a protocol, not a platform: a platform has an owner, and an owner can become the next place nobody can leave.

## After (in an essay)
> A platform is a shopping mall. The shops choose their windows and their stock, but the mall decides the hours, the rent and who gets a lease, and it can close a wing overnight. A protocol is the road network. Nobody owns the roads in that sense; there are rules about how to drive on them, and anyone can open a shop on any street. The analogy breaks at one point: the roads still have governors, and Polyphony's do too. What they cannot do is lock one shop's doors.

## Pitfalls
- An analogy the reader has to learn before it helps.
- Not saying where it breaks.
- Letting the analogy argue for things the mechanism doesn't support.
