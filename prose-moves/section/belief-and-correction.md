# Belief and correction

## Use when
- A widely held belief stands between the reader and the argument, and the text can show where it goes wrong.
- The correction is more interesting than the belief.

## Don't use when
- The belief is not widely held.
- The correction is only a matter of emphasis.

## Conflicts with
- [reframe](reframe.md) for the same paragraphs: correction keeps the question and fixes the answer; reframe changes the question.

## Tags
`certainty: calibrated` (works with flat if the correction is certain).

## Safe or adds
Safe if both belief and correction are in the source.

## The move
State the belief fairly and say why people hold it (it is usually partly true). Then show, with evidence or mechanism, where it stops being true. End with the corrected view in one plain sentence. The section-scale version of [name-the-picture-first](../document/name-the-picture-first.md).

## Before
> What accumulates is not a policy but a disposition — a publication that speaks with one voice and can point to no instance of having enforced it.

## After
> When a publication speaks with one voice, the usual explanation is that someone enforces it: an editor who spikes pieces, an owner with a line. Sometimes that is true. More often there is no instance to point to. The line is held by position, not argument, and it follows from the price of leaving.

## Pitfalls
- Caricaturing the belief.
- A correction that is just the opposite belief, equally unsupported.
