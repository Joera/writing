# Reframe

Variant: **question the frame** (below).

## Use when
- The usual framing of a problem leads to the wrong remedy (groupthink as a topic vs. groupthink as a structure).
- The text's remedy only makes sense once the question is changed.

## Don't use when
- The usual framing is fine and the text just disagrees with its answer: use [belief-and-correction](belief-and-correction.md).
- The reframe is cleverness with no consequence for the remedy.

## Conflicts with
- [belief-and-correction](belief-and-correction.md) and [two-step-diagnosis](two-step-diagnosis.md) for the same paragraphs.

## Tags
None.

## Safe or adds
Safe if the new frame is already the text's argument.

## The move
Name the usual frame in one sentence. Then name the frame the text uses instead, and show what changes when you look through it: a different cause, so a different remedy. Keep it to one turn; a reframe that needs a second reframe has failed.

**Question the frame (variant).** Instead of replacing the frame, ask what it takes for granted. Useful when the text's point is that a choice was never on the table (the publication assumed to be owned; the archive assumed to be the host's).

## Before
> The line is held by position, not argument, and no one had to choose that: it follows from the price of leaving.

## After
> The usual question is who is silencing dissent. The better question is what it costs to disagree. A herd is not held by a herder; it is held by a fence. Take down the fence.

*(The fence image is from the session's UnHerd discussion; under literal figuration it would be dropped and the reframe kept in plain words.)*

## Pitfalls
- Reframing so the opponent's view looks foolish.
- Reframes that are slogans.
- Under `figuration: literal`, a reframe that relies on a new image.
