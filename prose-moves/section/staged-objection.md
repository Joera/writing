# Staged objection

## Use when
- An informed reader will raise a specific objection at this point, and the text has an answer.
- The answer clarifies what the proposal is by saying what it is not (is this DRM? is this a platform?).

## Don't use when
- The objection is a straw man nobody would raise.
- The text has no good answer: state the limit instead ([edges-statement](edges-statement.md)).

## Conflicts with
- [mechanism-then-consequence](../document/mechanism-then-consequence.md) as the organising principle of the same section. A mechanism section may still stage one objection inside it.

## Tags
`persuasion: objection`. In an impersonal document, stage it without "you" ("An obvious question is…").

## Safe or adds
Safe if the answer is in the source. The objection itself is framing, not a claim.

## The move
State the objection in its strongest form, in a sentence the objector would accept. Then answer it with the mechanism, not with reassurance. If part of the objection holds, say which part.

## Before
> The encryption governs the offer, not the work, and it is access control on a public network, not a system for digital rights management.

## After
> An obvious question is whether this is digital rights management by another name. It is not. The encryption governs the offer, not the work: it controls which machinery may read the source, on a public network. The author's own copy stays on their machine, and they can send it to anyone.

## Pitfalls
- Weakening the objection so the answer looks better.
- Staging so many objections the section reads as defensive.
- In a "you" text, putting the objection in the reader's mouth when they would not raise it.
