# Cadence

## Use when
- The last sentence of a paragraph, section or document should land.
- The document's rhythm is cadenced, or cadenced at section closes.
- A sentence reads correctly but feels flat, rushed or clumsy when heard.

## Don't use when
- Mid-paragraph in plain technical prose, where clarity comes first.
- The sentence's order is fixed by meaning (a definition, a condition).

## Conflicts with
- [one-line-paragraph](../paragraph/one-line-paragraph.md) on the same sentence (one or the other carries the emphasis).

## Tags
`rhythm: cadenced` (or cadenced at section closes).

## Safe or adds
Safe: only order, length and sound change, never meaning.

## What cadence is
Cadence is how a sentence sounds as it ends, and how it sits against the sentences around it. Readers hear it even when reading silently. The implicit-prosody research shows silent readers project stress and phrasing onto text, and that rhythm affects how they parse it. So cadence is not only taste: a badly placed stress makes a sentence harder to understand, not just less pleasant.

## The move
Check six things, in order. The first two matter most.

1. **End weight.** The last position in a sentence gets the most stress, so put the newest or most important element there. Move familiar material forward and the new point to the end. "Disagreement has somewhere to go" lands; "Somewhere to go is what disagreement has" doesn't.
2. **A strong last word.** End on the stressed syllable of a content word, or on a stressed syllable followed by one or two light ones (a *falling* close: "by OWN-er-ship"). Avoid ending on light words: prepositions, pronouns, "too", "however", "as well", "in it".
3. **Length against the neighbours.** A close lands when its length differs from the sentence before it: a short sentence after a long one, or a long, even sentence after a run of short ones. Three sentences of similar length in a row make a drone.
4. **No stress pile-ups at the end.** Three or more stressed monosyllables in a row at the close ("this one big flaw") read as a stumble unless you want a blunt stop.
5. **No accidental meter or rhyme.** A run of regular da-DUM beats, or an internal rhyme, makes prose sound like verse and pulls attention from the meaning.
6. **Parallel closes match.** If two clauses are parallel, give their ends a similar rhythm and syllable count.

**How to test it.** Mark the stressed syllables in the last six to eight syllables and read them out. Then try one reordering that moves the key word to the end. Keep whichever ends on the stronger stress with fewer light syllables after it. A human reading aloud, or a text-to-speech read, is the final check ([read-aloud](../passes/read-aloud.md)).

## Before
> What the protocol cannot promise is a world without censorship, however.

## After
> It does not promise a world without censorship.

*(The light "however" goes, and the sentence ends on the stressed "cen-" followed by two light syllables, a falling close on the key word.)*

## Pitfalls
- Cadence that makes a sentence sound true rather than be true. Check it with the [epigram audit](../passes/epigram-audit.md).
- Cadence everywhere, which reads as incantation. Save it for closes.
- Reordering that moves the subject so far from its verb that the sentence gets harder to parse.
