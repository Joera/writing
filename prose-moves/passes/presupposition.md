# Presupposition

## What it checks
*What does the language take for granted?* Finds words and constructions that slip in a claim the text never makes, or a frame the text means to reject. Also: *are the costs hidden?*

## When to run
After rewriting, on cases, diagnoses and definitions.

## How
1. For each sentence, ask what must already be true for it to make sense. List those presuppositions.
2. For each, check: does the text claim it elsewhere? Does the author believe it? Is it fair to the people described?
3. Watch especially: possessives ("half the archive is *the other's*" presupposes ownership the case disputes), definite articles, "still", "even", "only", and words that carry a frame ("owner", "censor", "platform").
4. Check every case and example: does it have a life of its own (a specific situation, people who want something), or does it exist only to carry the point ([anti-pattern 4](../anti-patterns.md))? Props read as rigged.
5. List the costs of the proposal. Check each is stated somewhere, near the benefit it offsets ([cost-inside](../document/cost-inside.md)).
6. Ask who is **most exposed** when the system fails or is abused: the participant with the least money, standing or protection (a pseudonymous writer, a contributor facing a hostile holder). Check the text says what happens to them. *(Added from Octavia Butler's lens after the session; unreviewed.)*

## Output
A list: sentence · presupposition · claimed elsewhere? · fix or keep. Plus a list of costs and where each is stated, and who bears the worst of them.

## Example
- Case 1 draft: "half the archive is the other's" presupposes the archive is his. That is the dispute. → "half the archive is the other's work".
- "Fired" presupposes employment, which may mean the contract assigned rights. Keep, but the protocol does not override law: state that once.
