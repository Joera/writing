# Thought experiment

*A variant of [cases-first](cases-first.md).*

## Use when
- The argument depends on a situation that does not exist yet, or cannot be shown with a real case.
- Real cases would identify people, or would drag in disputes the text cannot settle.
- The reader needs to hold one variable fixed and watch what changes (same writers, different ownership).

## Don't use when
- Real cases exist and can be used safely: they are stronger evidence.
- The document's persuasion is pure mechanism and the reader expects no narrative.

## Conflicts with
- [thesis-up-top](thesis-up-top.md) for the same opening.
- [cases-first](cases-first.md) when both would open the same passage. A text may use real cases in one place and a thought experiment in another.

## Tags
`persuasion: thought experiment`

## Safe or adds
Adds. The scenario is new material; propose it in the plan.

## The move
Set up a situation explicitly as hypothetical ("Suppose…", "Imagine a publication where…"). Change one thing from the familiar world and follow the consequences honestly, including the ones that are inconvenient for the argument. End by stating what the experiment shows and what it does not.

## Before
> A falling-out costs the writer their income, their record, and the home of their byline; it costs the publication one contributor, who can be replaced.

## After
> Suppose the same falling-out, with one change: the archive belongs to the writer, and the site renders it only while both sides agree. The writer still loses the masthead. The publication now loses years of work it cannot replace. Nothing else has changed, and the balance has.

*(The scenario is new; it would need the author's approval.)*

## Pitfalls
- Rigging the scenario so only one outcome is possible. Le Guin's experiments follow costs that hurt her own side.
- Leaving the reader unsure whether the situation is real.
- Changing more than one variable, so the result proves nothing.
